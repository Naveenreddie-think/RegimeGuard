**Not yet the leakage-safe, walk-forward-validated regime series** — that's the
rolling-window stability check (item 4), which refits per-window with its own

### Grid (k ∈ {2,3,4,5} × λ ∈ {0, 10, 30, 50, 100, 200}) — full results: `regime_detection/results/jm_grid.csv`

  confirmed on our own data.

  2-state model) sits in a reasonable middle: 0.98–0.99 self-persistence, run lengths
  66–112 days, no collapsed states, across k=2..5.

## 2026-08-24 — Item 4 #3: refit / initialization stability — REAL INSTABILITY FOUND at k=4, k=5

Sequenced first per direction, as a gating check: "if regime assignments aren't stable
across random seeds, nothing else matters." It caught something. Full results:
`regime_detection/results/jm_refit_stability.csv`, `hmm_refit_stability.csv`.

**JM** (10 external `random_state` values, λ=50 anchor, ARI computed pairwise across
all 10 resulting label sequences):

| k | mean ARI | min ARI | max ARI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 3 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| **4** | **0.8747** | **0.7811** | 1.0000 |
| 5 | 0.6960 | 0.4975 | 1.0000 |

**HMM** (10 restarts per k, reusing `hmm_fit.py`'s own best-of-N selection data):

| k | mean ARI | min ARI | max ARI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.9907 | 0.9534 | 1.0000 |
| 3 | 0.8849 | 0.6764 | 1.0000 |
| 4 | 0.8901 | 0.7939 | 1.0000 |
| 5 | 0.8611 | 0.7267 | 1.0000 |

**Reading this straight, not softening it:** k=2 (both models) and JM's k=3 are
essentially perfectly stable across random seeds (ARI ≈ 1.0 everywhere). **k=4 — our
working anchor — is not.** JM's k=4 min ARI of 0.78 means at least one pair of
otherwise-identical fits (same data, same λ, only the top-level seed differs)
disagreed enough to be flagged, not just noisy at the margins. k=5 is worse for JM
(min ARI 0.50 — barely better than two unrelated clusterings agreeing by chance).
HMM's degradation pattern is broadly similar in shape but smaller in magnitude at
k=4/5, and additionally shows real degradation at k=3 (HMM min ARI 0.676) where JM's
k=3 was perfectly stable — the two models don't even agree on *where* the
instability starts.

**This directly challenges the (k=4, λ=50) anchor** used for every interpretability
read so far in this document. Not concluding a replacement value here — that's
exactly what this check exists to feed into the eventual formal (k, λ) decision
(rolling-window stability, still pending) — but the anchor can no longer be treated
as a safe, uncontroversial default while proceeding with the remaining checks.

**Not yet done:** investigating *why* k=4/k=5 destabilize (e.g., is there a specific
pair of features or a specific historical stretch driving the disagreement between
seeds?) — flagging as a real open question, not chasing it down unprompted before
next direction.

## 2026-08-24 — VIX ablation: VIX is NOT driving panic-day classification

Run at **k=3** (not k=4) specifically because the refit-stability check above found
k=4/k=5 seed-dependent, and running this ablation on an unstable k would have
confounded "does VIX matter" with "did we land on a different unstable seed." k=3 is
essentially perfectly stable (ARI 1.0), so a single-seed fit is well-justified here.
λ=50 anchor unchanged. Both fits (full 11-feature vs. ablated 9-feature/no-VIX) use
the identical 3900-date set — the ablated model was *not* given its naturally larger
available sample (Nifty alone doesn't need VIX and could cover 3 more dates), which
would have confounded the comparison with a sample-size difference. Full results:
`regime_detection/results/vix_ablation_summary.json`,
`vix_ablation_full_centers.csv`, `vix_ablation_ablated_centers.csv`.

**Clear answer: removing VIX barely changes anything.**

- Overall ARI between full and ablated label sequences: **0.9669**.
- The 9 shared (Nifty-return-derived) feature centers are nearly identical between
  the two fits (e.g., state 0's `ret_60`: 0.604 full vs 0.602 ablated; `DD_log_60`:
  −0.710 vs −0.717) — VIX's presence or absence barely perturbs how the return-based
  features get clustered.
- Stress-window assignment is **byte-identical** for 4 of 5 known windows (2016
  demonetization, 2018 IL&FS, 2020 COVID, 2022 rate-hike) — same dominant state, same
  exact percentages, down to the decimal. Only 2013 taper tantrum shifts slightly
  (92.5% → 86.25% in the dominant state).
- **COVID specifically: 100% of days land in the panic-like state in both the full
  and ablated models.** VIX being available or not makes zero difference to the
  clearest, most extreme historical event in the dataset.
- Worst-state (panic-like) membership: 1753 days (full) vs 1737 days (ablated),
  **1731 in common — Jaccard similarity 0.984.**

**Conclusion, stated plainly: at k=3/λ=50, the Nifty-return-derived features (return,
downside deviation, Sortino at 5/20/60-day horizons) are doing essentially all of the
real classification work on their own.** The earlier informal z-score read (return
features looked more extreme than VIX in the panic state) undersold this — the real
ablation shows VIX is close to redundant for *final regime classification* at this
setting, not just "not dominant."

**What this does and doesn't settle:** this answers whether VIX changes *which regime
a day gets assigned to*, once returns/vol are already known. It does **not** test
whether VIX-derived features (being forward-looking / market-implied rather than
backward-looking realized measures) might still carry earlier-warning value — e.g.
flagging a regime shift a day or two before the return-based features catch up. That's
a timing question, not a classification-agreement question, and this ablation wasn't
designed to answer it. Not investigating further here per direction — flagged as a
possible future question, not pursued now.

**Caveat inherited from the refit-stability finding:** this result is specific to
k=3, λ=50. Once rolling-window stability (still pending) settles the real (k, λ),
this ablation may be worth rerunning at whatever configuration is finally selected,
if it differs materially from k=3.

## 2026-08-24 — Formal HMM-vs-JM agreement (item 4 #6): low ARI/NMI, but a coherent story, not a contradiction

Run at k=3, full 11-feature set, same reasoning as the VIX ablation — comparing two
models at an unstable k (4 or 5) would confound "do the models agree" with "which
unstable local optimum did each land on." Full results:
`regime_detection/results/hmm_vs_jm_agreement.json`.

**Numbers, reported straight:**

- **ARI: 0.3098, NMI: 0.3353** — genuinely low agreement between the two models'
  label sequences, not a strong-agreement result.
- **JM: 31 transitions over the full period. HMM: 285 transitions** — roughly a 9x
  difference, directly reflecting the persistence gap already found in the refit-
  stability and first-fit checkpoints (JM's jump penalty vs. HMM having none).
- **Transition timing is asymmetric, and that asymmetry is informative:** JM
  transition → nearest HMM transition: median 5.0 days (JM's sparse transitions
  consistently land near *some* HMM transition). HMM transition → nearest JM
  transition: median 43.0 days (most HMM transitions have no nearby JM transition at
  all).

**Reading this, not just reporting it:** the low ARI/NMI is not a surprising
contradiction of earlier findings — it's the quantified consequence of something
already observed qualitatively (HMM's stress-window assignment "noisier/more split
than JM's", first HMM entry above) and structurally (HMM's much lower self-
persistence, refit-stability entry above). With HMM producing ~9x more transitions
than JM, the two label sequences necessarily diverge a lot in raw partition terms,
even if they're not disagreeing about the big picture. The transition-timing
asymmetry supports that reading directly: JM's sparse, high-conviction transitions
are consistently found near *a* HMM transition (suggesting JM is catching a subset of
real shifts HMM also detects), while HMM is additionally generating a large number of
transitions with no JM counterpart nearby — consistent with HMM flickering on
smaller, likely less meaningful fluctuations that JM's persistence penalty
deliberately suppresses.

**Not concluding "JM is right and HMM is wrong" from this alone** — that's exactly
the kind of thing the six-point checklist collectively, not any one check in
isolation, is supposed to establish. This result is one more piece of corroborating
evidence for the case already building (JM's persistence engineering does what it's
supposed to; HMM's lack of one produces real, measurable extra churn), not yet a
verdict.

**Remaining item 4 work: rolling-window stability** — the last and most expensive
check, which should now also inform whatever finally gets decided about (k, λ) given
everything found so far (k=4/5 instability, VIX near-redundancy at k=3, and this
agreement gap).

## 2026-08-24 — Rolling-window stability (item 4 #4): EVERY tested configuration fails the pre-registered bar

Design reviewed and confirmed before running (4 stress-event-anchored expanding-
window cutoffs, k∈{3,4,5} for both models, λ=50 fixed for JM, two-tier ARI bar
[interior ≥0.85 / edge ≥0.60] plus a state-aligned interpretability rule, single
cutoff failure disqualifies). State alignment between separately-fit models done via
centroid matching in a common (full-sample) standardized space +
`scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment`, not raw label comparison, per review before
running. Full results: `regime_detection/results/rolling_window_stability.json`.

**Ruled out an implementation artifact before trusting this result**: found and fixed
a real gap where this script's HMM fitting wasn't checking `.monitor_.converged`
(unlike `hmm_fit.py`'s version). Re-ran after fixing it - results were numerically
identical to the decimal, confirming the unconverged candidates weren't the ones
being selected in the original run. This is a real finding, not a convergence bug.

### Result: 0 of 6 configurations pass

| HMM | 3 | 0.60 / 0.57 | 0.60 / 0.54 | 0.63 / 0.80 | 0.65 / 0.76 (interp FAIL) | FAIL |

**Closest to passing**: JM k=3 at the 2022-12-31 cutoff — interior ARI 0.97 (comfortably
clears 0.85), interpretability passes, but edge ARI 0.36 misses the 0.60 bar. This is
the single best individual cell in the whole table; even it doesn't clear all three
bars.

**Most alarming single number**: JM k=3's edge ARI at the 2020-06-30 (COVID) cutoff is
**−0.08** — not just low, *negative*, meaning the truncated fit's labels for the last
90 trading days before that cutoff (roughly the COVID recovery window, Apr–Jun 2020)
actively disagree with the full-sample fit's labels for those exact same days, worse
than chance-level partition agreement would predict.

**Pattern, read honestly:** this isn't narrowly an edge-zone problem (which would have
been the more "forgivable" failure mode this design anticipated) — interior ARI is
below the 0.85 bar in nearly every cell too, often in the 0.4–0.7 range. Both models,
across all three tested k values, show real instability well into what should be the
"settled" part of history, not just at the informationally-hardest boundary.

**Every 2022-cutoff row that fails does so on the interpretability rule specifically**
(JM k=4, JM k=5, HMM k=3/4/5) — the 2022 rate-hike-volatility window's dominant regime,
once state-aligned, doesn't match between the truncated and full-sample fits. Given
the full-sample fit already showed this window as a genuine ~40/40 split across two
regimes (first JM entry, interpretability check), this may be a period where "dominant
regime" is inherently a fragile, near-tied statistic rather than a robust signal - worth
distinguishing from the flat-out ARI failures elsewhere, which look more like broad
instability.

### Open question this raises, not answered here

No configuration in the pre-registered search space is walk-forward stable by this
bar. Possible readings, not adjudicated here:
1. The bar itself may be miscalibrated (too strict) - though it was set *before*
   running, deliberately to avoid post-hoc rationalization, so loosening it now would
   need a principled reason, not just "nothing passed."
2. The per-window standardization refit (each cutoff gets its own clip+scale fit) may
   itself be a real source of instability - windows of very different length/
   composition could have meaningfully different feature distributions, shifting what
   "standardized" means enough to perturb cluster geometry, independent of any real
   regime change.
3. This could be a genuine, honest property of this dataset/feature set: 15 years of
   Indian equity market history spanning very different macro regimes (2010s low-rate
   era through 2020s post-COVID/rate-hike era) may simply not support a single
   walk-forward-stable regime model at daily granularity with this feature set.
4. The known instability sources already found (k=4/5 seed-instability) compound with
   this - though notably k=3 (JM) and the models generally don't cleanly escape this
   failure either, so seed-instability alone doesn't explain it.

**Not deciding among these here** - bringing the full result back for direction before
doing anything further, per the working process.

### Addendum — interpretability specifically is much stronger than "0/6" suggests

Pulled out as its own finding, per review, since it was at risk of getting lost under
the aggregate fail count: of the 24 individual (config × cutoff) interpretability
checks, **22 passed (91.7%)**. Five of six configurations (JM k=4/5, HMM k=3/4/5) fail
interpretability *only* at the 2022 cutoff - already understood as a fragile,
near-tied case (the full-sample fit itself shows this window as a genuine ~40/40
split, so "dominant regime" there is a fragile statistic even in the retrospective
fit, not a clean single answer to match against). The sixth (JM k=3) instead fails at
2016. **No configuration fails interpretability at more than one cutoff, and no
configuration fails at both 2020-COVID or 2018 - the two clearest, least-ambiguous
stress windows are correctly identified across every single tested configuration.**
This is a materially more encouraging signal than the overall "0/6 pass" framing
conveys on its own: the *characters* the models assign to states remain identifiable
and consistent across expanding windows far more often than the numeric ARI bars
alone would suggest. The failure driving "0/6" is concentrated in the ARI bars, not
broadly in whether the models understand what a regime represents.

## 2026-08-24 — Standardization-drift diagnostic: real, substantial drift found, concentrated exactly where the hypothesis predicted

Scoped narrowly per direction: isolate whether per-window standardization alone
(independent of clustering) explains part of the interior-ARI failures. Nothing
fixed - diagnostic only. Checked the 2016-12-31 and 2020-06-30 cutoffs specifically.
Full per-date output: `regime_detection/results/standardization_drift_2016-12-31.csv`,
`standardization_drift_2020-06-30.csv`.

**Method:** for each interior date (same >90-trading-day-before-cutoff definition as
the stability check), computed its standardized feature vector two ways - under the
truncated fit's own clip+scale pipeline, and under the full-sample fit's pipeline -
and measured the Euclidean distance between the two. This never runs the clustering;
it only asks whether the *numbers feeding it* are already different.

### Result: real, substantial drift - and it's biggest exactly where COVID isn't yet in the training data

**2016-12-31 cutoff** (COVID not in the training window):
- Scale ratios (full-sample scale ÷ truncated-fit scale) show real widening:
  **DD_log_60: 1.61x**, DD_log_20: 1.28x, vix_log: 1.18x, DD_log_5: 1.17x - the
  full-sample distribution is meaningfully wider for the volatility-related features
  once later data (COVID, 2022) is included.
- Mean per-date Euclidean distance between the two standardizations: **0.827**
  (median 0.743, max 1.560) - a non-trivial shift in an 11-dimensional standardized
  space where the earlier interpretability work showed inter-state centroid
  separations on a similar order of magnitude.
- Biggest per-feature contributors: `vix_log` (mean abs diff 0.375), `DD_log_60`
  (0.361), `DD_log_20` (0.294) - precisely the longer-horizon volatility/vol-level
  features, exactly what a volatility-spike event like COVID would be expected to
  widen most.

**2020-06-30 cutoff** (COVID *is* already in the training window):
- Scale ratios are close to 1.0 across the board (0.94-1.05) - once a window already
  contains COVID, adding 2022 on top barely changes the distributional scale further.
- Mean per-date Euclidean distance: **0.327** (median 0.318) - about **2.5x smaller**
  than the 2016 cutoff's drift.

**This directly corroborates the hypothesis, quantitatively, not just plausibly:**
the cutoff that doesn't yet contain COVID shows ~2.5x more standardization drift than
the cutoff that does - consistent with COVID specifically (not just "more data
generally") being the dominant driver of how much later data changes the yardstick
earlier dates get measured against. It also lines up with the original finding: the
2016 cutoff had the single worst interior ARI in the whole table (0.44, JM k=3),
while 2020-06-30's interior ARI (0.56, same model) was less bad, though still failing.

**What this does and doesn't settle:** this is real, quantified evidence that
per-window standardization is *at least part of* the mechanism behind the interior-
ARI failures - a genuine methodology artifact, not proof the underlying regime
concept itself is unstable. It does **not** by itself prove standardization drift is
the *entire* explanation (that would need rerunning the clustering with drift
controlled for, which wasn't in scope here - isolating the cause only, not fixing
anything). Bringing this back for direction on what to do next, per instruction.

## 2026-08-24 — Robust-scaling diagnostic (direction item #1): rules OUT scaler choice for HMM cleanly, partial/mixed picture for JM

Scoped narrowly per direction: swap only the standardization step (StandardScaler's
mean/std -> sklearn RobustScaler's median/IQR, default quantile_range=(25,75)),
clipping (3-sigma winsorization) left unchanged. Tested on JM k=3 and HMM k=3 (the
two best-characterized configs) across all 4 consecutive pairs. Stated expectation
going in, per direction: not expected to fully resolve the COVID-adjacent failures,
since COVID looks like a sustained distributional shift, not a few outlier days a
robust scaler would neutralize - reporting the actual result regardless.

**Verified two suspicious-looking results directly before trusting them, same
discipline as the rest of this investigation:**
1. Confirmed `RobustScalerPD` genuinely produces different transformed values than
   `StandardScalerPD` on the same clipped data (max abs difference 0.93) - the
   scaler swap itself isn't a no-op.
2. HMM's pairwise ARI results came back **numerically identical to the StandardScaler
   baseline for all 4 pairs** - suspicious enough to check directly rather than
   report as "no effect" on faith. Traced it down: raw single-fit labels genuinely
   differ in 857 of 1514 positions between the two scalers, but this is a pure label-
   permutation artifact - **ARI between the StandardScaler-fit and RobustScaler-fit
   labels for the *same* window is exactly 1.0**, meaning the underlying partition
   (which dates cluster together) is mathematically identical; only the arbitrary
   EM state-index numbering differs. HMM with diagonal covariance is genuinely
   invariant to this specific scaler swap on this data, after clipping. **This
   cleanly rules out scaler choice as a factor in HMM's instability** - whatever
   drives it is something else entirely, not this.

### JM results: real, substantive, and mixed - not a clean fix

| Pair | Interior ARI: Std → Robust | Edge ARI: Std → Robust |
|---|---|---|
| 2016→2018 | 0.98 → **0.35** (PASS→FAIL) | 1.00 → **0.25** (PASS→FAIL) |
| 2018→2020 | 0.95 → **0.37** (PASS→FAIL) | 0.37 → **0.91** (FAIL→PASS) |
| **2020→2022 (COVID)** | 0.55 → **0.86** (FAIL→PASS) | −0.08 → −0.07 (still FAIL) |
| 2022→full | 0.97 → 0.90 (still PASS) | 0.36 → **0.00** (still FAIL) |

**Reading this straight:** robust scaling does *not* cleanly resolve the COVID-
adjacent failures, matching the stated expectation. The 2020→2022 pair's interior
ARI does improve meaningfully (0.55→0.86, converting a fail to a pass) - a real,
partial confirmation that some of the interior instability there is standardization-
sensitive. But that same pair's catastrophic edge ARI is essentially unchanged
(−0.08→−0.07) - the edge-zone disagreement is not a standardization artifact.

**The more important finding: robust scaling actively destabilizes the previously-
best-performing pair.** 2016→2018 flips from a clean pass (both bars) to failing
both - a real cost, not just "no effect elsewhere." Whatever makes JM's clustering
sensitive to this specific choice of scaler cuts both ways: it can help at the
specific pair it was hoped to help, and hurt at pairs that weren't broken.

**Overall verdict:** ruling in/out, as scoped - **ruled out** as a factor for HMM
entirely (mathematically invariant). **Partially ruled in** for JM, but not as a fix
to adopt: it trades one pair's instability for another's, netting out no better than
the original StandardScaler choice when judged across the whole chain. Not proposing
a scaler change as a result of this - the diagnostic answered the question asked
(is this part of the mechanism) without producing a clean remediation.

## 2026-08-24 — Recalibration design: APPROVED

Directly answers proposal §9's open question ("retraining cadence and how drift into
a new regime will be detected and handled operationally"), grounded in this session's
findings rather than speculative. Approved as proposed, with one scoping note added
below per review.

**1. Trigger — schedule as a floor, drift-detection as an early-warning layer, not
either alone.** Two-tier monitoring: (a) cheap, continuous standardization-drift
metric (reusing `standardization_drift.py`'s Euclidean-distance-in-standardized-space
approach) as a first-line signal; (b) when (a) crosses a threshold, a confirmatory
"shadow fit" + ARI check against the live model (reusing
`rolling_window_stability(_consecutive).py`'s methodology) before treating it as a
real trigger. No cadence number proposed yet - honestly flagged as unvalidated
(everything tested so far, 1.5-3.6 years, showed real instability somewhere; nothing
shorter was tested until the follow-up below). Governance: a trigger flags a
recalibration *for review*, doesn't silently swap the live model, consistent with
this project's human-in-the-loop stance on consequential actions.

**2. Historical labels — point-in-time-honest, versioned, never overwritten.**
Directly reuses the `daily_bars`/`superseded_by` pattern already established in
Phase 1: new `regime_labels` table (trade_date, model_version_id, regime, labeled_at,
superseded_by) and `model_versions` table (one row per recalibration event, mirroring
`ingestion_runs`' audit role), plus a `current_regime_labels` view for "what do we
believe now." Recalibration adds a new version and a full new label set; it never
mutates history. Necessity, not just tidiness: the entire rolling-window methodology
this session built depends on being able to reconstruct "what would the model have
said with only data through date X" after the fact - silently overwriting history
would destroy the exact evidence this investigation exists to examine.

**Scoping note added per review, not a permanent constant:** each recalibration
event refits on the *full* available history (not a partial/incremental relabel) -
the right call now, since every fit this session ran in low single-digit minutes at
15 years of daily data. This is an explicit scoping decision appropriate to the
current data horizon, not something to silently assume holds forever - if this
system is ever still running decades from now, full-refit-every-time may need
revisiting (e.g., bounded-window refits) purely on compute-cost grounds, independent
of anything found in this investigation.

**3. Feeds the confidence/abstention layer directly - a real extension of the
proposal, not just plumbing.** Time-since-last-fit and live standardization drift
become explicit inputs to the regime-conditioned confidence score (a staleness
discount), plus a hard circuit-breaker abstention if drift exceeds a severe
threshold - a concrete, evidence-based instance of the proposal's "know when not to
trust itself" principle, not just a general aspiration.

**Next**: run the short-gap (3mo/6mo) pairwise-ARI follow-up flagged in §1, using the
existing consecutive-pair machinery, to inform the cadence floor with real evidence
before implementing anything.

## 2026-08-24 — Short-gap (3mo/6mo) follow-up: interior stability essentially solved at short cadence; edge-zone risk during live crises does not go away with any cadence

Tested 4 reference starting points (same cutoffs already characterized) x 2 gap
sizes (+3 months, +6 months) x 2 models (JM, HMM), k=3, same ARI bars as every other
check in this investigation. ARI-only (no interpretability check - most short-gap
endpoints don't land on a clean stress-window boundary). Full results:
`regime_detection/results/short_gap_stability.csv`.

### Result: 5/8 pass at both gap sizes - a dramatic improvement over the long gaps

| Gap | Pass rate | Mean interior ARI | Mean edge ARI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 months | 5/8 | **0.978** | 0.765 |
| 6 months | 5/8 | **0.965** | 0.765 |

**Interior ARI is at or near 1.0 for nearly every pair tested** (nine of sixteen
cells are exactly 1.0; the rest are 0.89-0.998) - a stark contrast with the 1.5-3.6
year gaps, where interior ARI regularly fell to 0.4-0.7. **This directly answers the
cadence question with real evidence**: whatever is causing the interior-stability
failures at long gaps is a long-gap phenomenon - at 3-6 month horizons it is close to
resolved.

**Where it still fails, and why this isn't a cadence problem:** every failure is an
edge-zone failure, and every failing edge zone is one that itself covers an unusually
extreme stretch:
- **2020-06-30 fails at both gap sizes, both models** (JM edge ARI 0.57, HMM 0.19) -
  that specific 90-day edge window covers the COVID crash itself (Feb-Jun 2020). No
  amount of *recalibration frequency* changes the fact that a freshly-computed edge
  zone sitting inside the crash itself is inherently unstable - there simply isn't
  enough post-crash context yet for the classification to have settled, regardless of
  when the next refit happens.
- **2022-12-31 fails for JM at both gap sizes** (edge ARI 0.356, identical at 3mo and
  6mo) - consistent with the already-known ~40/50 ambiguity in that window. Notably,
  **HMM passes this one cleanly** (edge ARI 1.0) - a real model-specific difference
  worth carrying forward, not previously this clear-cut.

### What this settles for the recalibration design

**Interior drift is a solved problem at short cadence** - supports a cadence floor in
the quarterly range (3 months tested cleanly; 6 months only marginally worse) rather
than anything close to annual. **Edge-zone risk during a live, ongoing extreme event
is not something cadence can fix** - this is exactly what the confidence/abstention
circuit-breaker (recalibration design §3) is for, not a cadence problem: the system
should recognize "we are currently near the edge of what any model, however
recently refit, can classify confidently" and discount/abstain accordingly, rather
than trusting a freshly-computed classification of the last 90 days at face value
just because the model is up to date.

**Still not a fully validated specific cadence number** - "quarterly" is now
supported by real evidence at 3 and 6 months specifically, not proposed as a round
number. Gaps between 1 week and 3 months, and between 6 months and 1.5 years, remain
untested - reasonable to treat quarterly as the recommended floor without closing
the book on refining it further later.

### Cleanup: demo model_versions removed from the real DB

The two demo model_versions (id 1, 2) and their labels were validation scaffolding,
not real recalibration events - left in place, `get_active_model_version` would

### Threshold recalibration: TIER1_DRIFT_THRESHOLD grounded in real evidence, not a guess

Re-checked the standardization-drift entry above before changing the number, rather
than accepting "0.827 known-bad vs. 0.327 known-fine" at face value: **neither
reference point is actually a passing configuration.** The 2016-12-31 cutoff (drift
0.827) had the worst interior ARI in the whole rolling-window table (0.44); the
2020-06-30 cutoff (drift 0.327) was less catastrophic but still failed the same 0.85

metric to anchor a low end against.

Given that, `TIER1_DRIFT_THRESHOLD` moved from the placeholder **0.5** to **0.3** -
below the milder of the two known-bad points, so tier 1 fires even on the
less-severe known failure case rather than only the catastrophic one. Corroborating
cross-check: tier 1's own metric, computed a different way (stored-vs-fresh scaler on
the most recent 90 days of live data, vs. standardization_drift.py's truncated-vs-
full-sample comparison on interior dates), measured 0.335 for a model stale at the
same 2020-06-30 cutoff - within 2.5% of standardization_drift.py's 0.327 for that
exact cutoff, despite the methodological difference. Still a first cut pending real
operational drift observations, not treated as final.
